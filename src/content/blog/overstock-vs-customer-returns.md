## A clearance label is only the beginning

Overstock is inventory beyond a business’s current needs. Customer returns are products that came back from customers. Packaging-damaged stock has an issue with its packaging. **These descriptions can overlap, and none tells the complete story of a product’s present condition.**

That distinction matters when you are preparing a lot for a bulk buyer. Clear descriptions make the conversation more specific and reduce the chance that two teams are discussing different versions of the same stock.

## Explain why the inventory is available

Businesses create surplus for many reasons. A collection reaches the end of its season. A range is discontinued. Sales differ from a forecast. A channel stops carrying a product.

These circumstances explain the stock’s origin. They do not automatically establish a product defect. A discontinued item may still be unopened and complete. Equally, a product in a sealed outer carton may still need inspection before any condition statement can be made.

For returned stock, be as specific as your records allow. Distinguish the recorded return reason from what your team has verified about the item.

## Describe what is known today

A useful condition description is factual and limited to the checks actually performed. Avoid broad labels such as “perfect,” “like new” or “fully working” when the evidence is a visual check of a small sample.

These hypothetical examples show how origin and condition can be recorded independently:

| Stock origin | Known condition | Detail worth sharing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| End-of-season apparel | Unused, tags present | Size and colour mix |
| Customer return | Tested working | Outer box damaged |
| Customer return | Untested | Charger missing |
| Range discontinuation | Packaging affected | Product not yet inspected |

There is no universal meaning for every commercial grade used by inventory businesses. If you use an internal grading system, include the definitions with the stock list.

[Liquidation.com’s seller requirements](https://www.liquidation.com/c/seller/index.html) illustrate why accurate product descriptions, condition information and manifests matter in bulk inventory transactions.

## Group stock so the differences remain visible

Mixed lots can be a valid buying opportunity. The issue is not that every item must be identical; it is whether the buyer can understand the mix.

Separate meaningful differences into distinct rows or groups. For example, put tested and untested electronics in separate groups. For kitchen sets, identify complete and incomplete sets. For apparel, distinguish damaged labels from damaged fabric.

When counts are estimates, call them estimates. When inspection is based on sampling, record that basis. Avoid letting a neat spreadsheet create an impression of certainty that your underlying records do not support.

## Keep the offer discussion specific to the lot

Product mix, quantities, condition, completeness and location all help frame a buying conversation. Original retail price alone does not determine a purchase offer.

Before agreeing a transaction, make sure both sides understand which stock is included, which items are excluded and what further checks are needed. Payment and pickup details should be discussed for the actual lot rather than inferred from a general website statement.

For a seller, the practical objective is simple: a shared description of the inventory and a clear next step.
